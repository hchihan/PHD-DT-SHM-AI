The safest, standards‑aligned way to define your thesis “Digital Twin maturity level” is as a **data‑informed, monitoring‑oriented capability level** that sits below predictive simulation and autonomous control, using terminology from ISO/IEC 30186 and Gemini‑principles‑based research on asset‑management digital twins.[^1][^2][^3]

Below I identify suitable frameworks, filter out those that mandate predictive/autonomous features, and then propose defensible wording you can reuse in the thesis.

***

## 1. Core authoritative frameworks you can rely on

### 1.1 ISO/IEC 30186:2025 – Digital twin maturity model and assessment

- **Type:** NORMATIVE (international standard, generic DT maturity).
- **Scope:** Provides a generic digital twin maturity model, assessment indicators, and guidance for maturity assessment across domains.[^2][^4][^5]
- **Maturity levels:** Uses six levels (0–5): 0 Pre‑DT, 1 Initial, 2 Managed, 3 Defined, 4 Quantitatively Managed, 5 Optimised.[^6]
- **Key dimensions:** Convergence, Capability, Integrated View, Time, Trustworthiness – these indicators focus on how well data are integrated, how consistently the twin supports decision‑making, and how reliable insights are.[^6]
- **Relevance to your thesis:**
    - Early‑mid levels (Initial/Managed/Defined) describe data‑integrated monitoring and decision support, not mandatory physics‑based predictive modelling. The standard is generic and does not require calibrated FEM or autonomous control for something to be called a Digital Twin.[^5][^2]
    - “Quantitatively Managed” and “Optimised” can include advanced analytics and automation, but these are **upper levels**, not a universal requirement.[^6]

**Reason for inclusion:**
ISO/IEC 30186 gives you a normative, vendor‑neutral maturity ladder that you can cite to justify that a monitoring‑and‑decision‑support twin is a legitimate maturity stage even without predictive simulation. This anchors your thesis vocabulary in an international standard.[^4][^2][^5]

***

### 1.2 ISO/IEC 30173:2023 – Concepts and terminology for Digital Twins

- **Type:** NORMATIVE (terminology and conceptual framework).
- **Scope:** Defines core DT concepts, life‑cycle processes, functional views, and stakeholders.[^3]
- **Key points:**
    - Recognises different **types of digital twin** and life‑cycle views; not all DTs are fully predictive or autonomous.[^3]
    - Emphasises digital twin as a socio‑technical system combining data, models, and stakeholders across the asset life cycle (including operations and maintenance).[^3]

**Reason for inclusion:**
You can use ISO/IEC 30173 to define what a “digital twin system” is and then position your maturity level as one **type** within that concept, focused on descriptive monitoring and asset‑management support rather than prediction.[^3]

***

### 1.3 Gemini Principles‑based Digital Twin Maturity Model for Asset Management (Chen et al., Sustainability 2021)

- **Citation:** Chen et al., “Gemini Principles‑Based Digital Twin Maturity Model for Asset Management,” *Sustainability* 13(8224), 2021, DOI 10.3390/su13158224.[^1]
- **Type:** DIRECT EVIDENCE (project‑level DT maturity for built assets and asset management).
- **Study object:** Real asset‑management DT implementations, including Shanghai Tower and a Cambridge campus building.[^1]
- **Framework:**
    - Three main dimensions derived from the Gemini Principles: **purpose**, **trust**, **function**.[^1]
    - Nine sub‑dimensions and 27 rubrics covering project objectives, governance, data quality, interoperability, and evolution.[^1]
    - A rubric‑based scoring scheme mapped to five maturity stages for DT‑enabled asset management: Unaware, Identifiable, Aware, Communicative, Interactive, Instructive and Intelligent.[^1]
- **Maturity stages that match your thesis level:**
    - **Aware / Communicative:** Assets are identifiable and registered; IoT devices partially integrated; digital models used for monitoring; ontology and standards for data sharing emerging.[^1]
    - **Interactive:** Ontology clearly defined; methods implemented for information retrieval and asset integration; shareable knowledge and value are provided (e.g., dashboards, anomaly flags for maintenance).[^1]

These stages explicitly focus on **data integration, monitoring, and decision support** rather than mandatory predictive simulation or autonomous control; “Instructive and Intelligent” is where semi‑automatic or automatic decision making appears.[^1]

**Reason for inclusion:**
This paper directly operationalises DT maturity for **asset management and O\&M**, aligns with the UK Gemini Principles, and provides a peer‑reviewed rubric for “interactive” monitoring twins that support maintenance decisions without requiring physics‑based prediction.[^1]

***

### 1.4 Smart Maintenance Maturity for Constructed Assets (Johannes et al., J. Constr. Eng. Manage., 2021)

- **Citation:** Johannes et al., “Identifying Maturity Dimensions for Smart Maintenance Management of Constructed Assets: A Multiple Case Study,” *Journal of Construction Engineering and Management* 147(9):05021007, 2021, DOI 10.1061/(ASCE)CO.1943‑7862.0002112.[^7]
- **Type:** METHODOLOGICAL EVIDENCE (maturity of digitalised maintenance, not DT per se).
- **Study object:** Smart maintenance in corporate facilities management (university campuses, mixed building portfolios).[^7]
- **Framework:**
    - Eight higher‑level maturity dimensions and 23 lower‑level dimensions, including **tracking and tracing**, asset data governance, contractor collaboration, and knowledge management.[^7]
    - Focus on how organisations use networked asset data (including sensors and digital registers) to support maintenance and asset management.[^7]
- **Relevance:**
    - Although it is not a “digital twin” maturity model, the smart‑maintenance dimensions describe the same **data‑driven, human‑in‑the‑loop maintenance processes** your thesis is interested in: capturing condition data, ensuring traceability of work orders, and supporting maintenance decisions.[^7]
    - The framework stays largely at descriptive and organisational capability levels; predictive analytics are discussed but not imposed as a minimum requirement.[^7]

**Reason for inclusion:**
Use this as methodological evidence that mature, data‑rich maintenance systems can be evaluated on dimensions like traceability, governance, and collaboration without needing predictive physics models, reinforcing your “data‑informed” positioning.[^7]

## 2. Frameworks to treat as adjacent or out‑of‑scope

To stay within your “limited FEM” and non‑predictive focus, you should **not** adopt maturity frameworks whose baseline definition of “digital twin” requires predictive simulation, autonomous control, or fully calibrated physics.

From the review and related industry commentary:[^7][^1]

- **Smart Energy International and similar evolution schemes** that classify DT evolution as reporting → analysing → predicting → integrating → prescribing → autonomous decisioning were noted as high‑level descriptions with no formal maturity model and a strong emphasis on prediction and autonomy.[^1]
    - **Classification:** OUT OF SCOPE for a thesis that wants to legitimise non‑predictive DTs.
- **Industry 4.0‑style smart maintenance maturity models** (e.g., manufacturing‑centric CMMI variants) often assume predictive analytics and autonomous optimisation as essential features for “smartness.”[^7]
    - **Classification:** STRONG ADJACENT – useful background, but unsafe to anchor your definition because they blur “digital twin” with predictive/autonomous systems.
- **Lab‑centric calibrated FEM‑based twins for structural health monitoring** (not in the two papers above but common in the DT literature) typically identify “true DT” with calibrated physics models and automated diagnosis.
    - **Classification:** OUT OF SCOPE for your charter (contradicts “FEM is nominal, uncalibrated, reference‑only”).

You can still cite these as examples of **higher maturity levels** or future work but avoid using their definitions as the baseline for “Digital Twin maturity” in your thesis.

## 3. Mapping your thesis level to existing maturity terminology

Your thesis wants a defensible level described as:

> “data‑informed, descriptive monitoring, component‑level traceability, and human‑in‑the‑loop inspection support.”

This can be mapped to the existing frameworks as follows.

### 3.1 Alignment to ISO/IEC 30186 levels

Interpreting your target level against the 0–5 ladder:[^2][^6]

- **Corresponds to:** level 2–3 range (“Managed” / “Defined”).
    - **Data‑informed:** DT capability systematically integrates data sources to support decisions (Managed).[^6]
    - **Descriptive monitoring:** Twin provides an integrated view of time‑stamped asset state (Defined), but not necessarily predictive simulation.[^6]
    - **Component‑level traceability:** Convergence and Integrated View indicators are satisfied at asset/component granularity; work orders and sensor data are linked to specific components.[^6]
    - **Human‑in‑the‑loop inspection support:** Time and Trustworthiness dimensions emphasise data timeliness and quality, with humans still making final judgements.[^6]

You can therefore describe your thesis level as:

> “An ISO/IEC 30186‑aligned Digital Twin capability at the ‘Managed/Defined’ maturity range, focusing on converged asset data, integrated descriptive monitoring, and trusted decision support for human inspection.”[^2][^6]

This makes clear that predictive simulation and autonomy correspond to **higher levels (Quantitatively Managed, Optimised)**, beyond the thesis scope.

***

### 3.2 Alignment to Gemini‑based DT Asset‑Management maturity (Chen et al.)

From Chen et al.’s five stages for asset‑management DTs:[^1]

- **Your level is best aligned with ‘Interactive’** (score range 27–36) in their rubric:
    - Assets and data shareable in a standardised format (ontology defined, open schema where appropriate).[^1]
    - Methods for information retrieval and asset integration implemented (e.g., BIM/FM integration, sensor data mapped to asset IDs).[^1]
    - Digital twin provides **shareable knowledge and value** such as dashboards, alerts, and anomaly flags to support maintenance and O\&M decisions.[^1]

They explicitly distinguish “Instructive and Intelligent” as the stage where semi‑automatic/automatic management and intelligent decision support appear.[^1]

So you can write:

> “Within the Gemini‑principles‑based asset‑management maturity model, the thesis targets the ‘Interactive’ stage: a digital twin that integrates asset and sensor data, supports standardised information sharing and anomaly detection, and provides human‑interpretable insights for inspection and maintenance, without requiring instructive or intelligent autonomous control.”[^1]

***

### 3.3 Alignment to smart‑maintenance maturity (Johannes et al.)

Your level can be linked to selected higher‑level dimensions:[^7]

- **Data governance / asset register as single source of truth:** Supports component‑level traceability of assets and maintenance actions.[^7]
- **Tracking and tracing of jobs and data flows:** Ensures inspection records and maintenance actions can be mapped to specific components and digital objects.[^7]
- **Culture, leadership, alignment:** Supports human‑in‑the‑loop inspection and decision‑making using digital information.[^7]

These dimensions describe an organisation where digital data are collected, structured, and used to **inform maintenance decisions**, but where human judgement remains central and predictive analytics are not mandatory.[^7]

You can refer to this as:

> “A smart‑maintenance maturity level characterised by internalised data ownership, traceable asset registers, and digitally supported—but still human‑led—inspection and maintenance workflows for constructed assets.”[^7]

## 4. Recommended terminology for the thesis

Based on the standards and peer‑reviewed models above, the safest terminology for your thesis is:

### 4.1 Name of the maturity level

Use a name that clearly signals **descriptive, data‑integrated, human‑centred** capability and avoids implying prediction or autonomy:

- **Preferred term:**
    - “Interactive **data‑informed Digital Twin** for asset management” (borrowing “Interactive” from Chen et al.).[^1]
- Acceptable alternatives (depending on your writing style):
    - “Managed/Defined Digital Twin monitoring capability for built assets” (anchored in ISO/IEC 30186 levels).[^2][^6]
    - “Descriptive monitoring Digital Twin for operations and maintenance.”

All three can be justified with citations; you could define the level formally in your methodology chapter and then consistently use one phrase.

### 4.2 Formal definition text you can adapt

A concise, defensible definition consistent with the sources could be:

> “For this thesis, a Digital Twin at **Interactive data‑informed maturity** is defined as a socio‑technical system that (i) maintains a converged, component‑level asset data model linked to sensor and inspection records; (ii) provides time‑stamped, descriptive monitoring of asset condition and states; and (iii) supports human‑in‑the‑loop inspection and maintenance decisions through traceable visualisation and analytics, without requiring predictive simulation, autonomous control, or calibrated physics‑based models as baseline capabilities.”[^2][^3][^7][^1]

You can then position this definition:

- As **ISO‑aligned** (Managed/Defined levels), with predictive/autonomous behaviours reserved for higher maturity.[^2][^6]
- As **Gemini‑aligned** (Interactive stage, not Instructive/Intelligent).[^1]
- As consistent with **smart‑maintenance maturity** dimensions for constructed assets (data governance, traceability, human decision‑making).[^7]
