# 9. Find peer-reviewed studies on transparent, human-in-the-loop decision support

for condition-based inspection of buildings or civil infrastructure.

Prioritize frameworks that translate monitoring evidence into:
Normal / Watch / Inspect / Priority Inspection,
or equivalent escalation levels.

Extract:

- evidence inputs;
- escalation rules;
- role of expert or facility manager;
- inspection trigger;
- action-status workflow;
- audit trail;
- validation by scenarios, expert review, or facility managers.

Exclude:

- autonomous maintenance;
- RUL and failure-time prediction;
- optimization-only studies;
- complex weighted health indices;
- systems that automatically declare structural damage.

There are a few relevant peer‑reviewed, human‑in‑the‑loop decision support frameworks for condition‑based inspection and maintenance of buildings and civil infrastructure, but most use broad condition indices rather than explicit Normal / Watch / Inspect / Priority ladders. Below are selected studies that come closest, with the requested extraction and classification.

## 1. Building Maintenance Decision Support System (BMDSS) – Langevine et al., 2006

**Citation**
Langevine, R., Allouche, M., AbouRizk, S. “Decision Support Tool for the Maintenance Management of Buildings.” Joint International Conference on Computing and Decision Making in Civil and Building Engineering, Montréal, 2006.[^1]

**Classification**
METHODOLOGICAL EVIDENCE (for portfolio‑level human‑in‑the‑loop maintenance decision support in buildings).

**Study object**
Large portfolios of public buildings decomposed into systems, components, and elements using Uniformat II; inspections at element level feeding building‑level condition assessment and MR\&R planning.[^1]

**Evidence inputs**

- Periodic, detailed inspection ratings at element level on a discrete ordinal scale (e.g. A–F condition performance rating).[^1]
- Hierarchical building inventory (systems → components → elements) following Uniformat II, with expert‑elicited importance weights via AHP.[^1]
- Cost estimates for alternative maintenance / rehabilitation / replacement (MR\&R) actions per component.[^1]
- Expert‑defined Markov transition probability matrices describing deterioration between condition states when no intervention occurs.[^1]

**Escalation / prioritisation rules**
The system does not publish a literal Normal / Watch / Inspect ladder, but implements an explicit prioritisation logic that plays the same role:[^1]

- Elements are rated on a multi‑state condition scale; lower states represent more severe deterioration (state F is “absorbing” until repair).
- AHP‑derived weights and condition ratings are combined to produce a weighted condition index per component and system, creating ordered lists of “priority” components.
- Components in poor states with high weights and unfavourable deterioration forecasts are candidates for higher‑priority MR\&R actions in the short‑term optimization module.[^1]
In practice this can be interpreted as:
- Good condition + low criticality → “Normal”.
- Moderate deterioration or near intervention threshold → “Watch”.
- Poor condition with approaching absorbing state or high criticality → “Inspect / Plan MR\&R”.
- Very poor condition plus critical system and budget feasibility → “Priority intervention”.

**Role of expert / facility manager (human‑in‑the‑loop)**

- Experts define the deterioration transition matrices where empirical data are lacking; these matrices encode their judgment of how quickly specific components deteriorate.[^1]
- Asset managers select feasible MR\&R alternatives for each component (replacement, major rehabilitation, minor rehabilitation, marginal repair) and adjust them based on practical constraints.[^1]
- Facility managers interpret inspection results, validate the derived condition indexes, and decide whether to override or accept model suggestions in light of safety, usability, and policy considerations.[^1]
- Expert judgment is explicitly used to set AHP weights for criteria and components, influencing the priority ranking of assets.[^1]

**Inspection trigger**

- Triggered by the standardized “building condition assessment program” that schedules inspections at element level; deteriorating components (via forecasts or low condition rating) drive more frequent or detailed inspections.[^1]
- Components reaching specific condition thresholds (e.g., approaching state F or passing a predefined “level of intervention”) prompt intervention planning rather than automatic decisions.[^1]

**Action‑status workflow**
Modules and workflow (all human‑supervised):[^1]

- **Condition Assessment Module**:
    - Inspection data acquisition.
    - Recording of executed maintenance actions.
    - Generation of current and projected performance trends and scenario graphs.
- **Budget Forecasting Module**:
    - Forecasts future maintenance budgets based on projected deterioration and planned actions.
- **Short‑Term Optimization Module**:
    - Within an annual budget constraint, selects MR\&R actions for components to maximize overall building condition.
    - Asset managers can change options or priorities before implementation.[^1]
Actions pass from “candidate” to “selected” to “executed,” with the system tracking condition response (e.g., deterioration curve before/after rehabilitation). Figures show deterioration curves pre‑ and post‑rehabilitation to support human evaluation.[^1]

**Audit trail / transparency**

- Hierarchical decomposition and standardized rating scales make inspection evidence traceable from building index down to individual elements.[^1]
- The Markov deterioration modeling and AHP weighting are documented; decisions can be traced to input ratings, transition probabilities, and chosen MR\&R options.[^1]
- The condition assessment and optimization modules record both current and predicted conditions plus executed actions, providing a time‑stamped history of decisions and their effects.[^1]

**Validation**

- Methodological validation via references to prior applications of Markov chains and AHP in pavements, bridges, and building maintenance.[^1]
- Example deterioration curves and case‑type discussion illustrating plausible behavior of elements before and after rehabilitation, which can be reviewed by experts for face validity.[^1]
- No formal quantitative scenario testing with end‑users is reported, but the framework is grounded in accepted infrastructure management practice.

**Directly supported claim and limitation**

- **Claim**: A hierarchical, inspection‑driven, human‑supervised DSS can structure building maintenance information and support cost‑effective MR\&R decisions under budget constraints without automatically declaring structural damage.[^1]
- **Limitations**:
    - Heavy reliance on expert judgment when empirical deterioration data are missing.
    - The optimization module introduces cost‑benefit prioritization but not explicit 4‑level escalation labels.
    - Focus is on whole‑building portfolios rather than component‑level vibration or advanced SHM inputs.
**Reason for inclusion**: Provides a transparent, human‑in‑the‑loop DSS architecture for condition‑based building maintenance using inspection ratings and expert rules, closely aligning with your interest in escalation and workflow without autonomous damage diagnosis.[^1]

## 2. Integrated DSS for Corrective, Preventive, and Condition‑Based Maintenance (FMM DSS) – ASCE Proceedings, 2010/2012

**Citation**
“A Decision Support System for Integrating Corrective Maintenance, Preventive Maintenance, and Condition‑Based Maintenance.” ASCE Proceedings, Facilities Maintenance Management (FMM) framework, published online 2012.[^2][^3]

**Classification**
METHODOLOGICAL EVIDENCE (integrated, BIM‑linked, condition‑based maintenance workflow with human oversight).

**Study object**
Facilities maintenance management for buildings, integrating asset management, real‑time project management, condition monitoring systems, and building information models into a Web‑based prototype.[^3][^2]

**Evidence inputs**

- Manually entered corrective maintenance (CM) work orders from facility staff.[^2][^3]
- System‑generated preventive maintenance (PM) orders based on schedules.
- Condition monitoring system outputs (e.g., sensor data or inspection‑based condition assessments) linked to assets in BIM.[^3][^2]
- Resource data (equipment, materials, trades, contractors, tools) for allocation and planning.[^2][^3]

**Escalation / prioritisation rules**
The paper emphasizes normalization and integration rather than explicit 4‑level labels, but the workflow implies escalation stages:[^3][^2]

- All maintenance sources (CM, PM, CBM) are normalized as “projects and tasks,” allowing comparisons by urgency, condition, and resource requirements.[^2][^3]
- Condition‑based maintenance tasks are generated when monitored asset conditions cross predefined thresholds or trends, effectively shifting items from “normal” monitoring to “condition‑triggered” tasks.
- Resource allocation algorithms prioritize tasks given constraints; higher‑risk or more deteriorated assets and critical functions receive earlier scheduling, which operationally approximates “priority inspection/maintenance.”[^3][^2]

**Role of expert / facility manager (human‑in‑the‑loop)**

- Facility managers review and enter CM orders, confirm PM and CBM tasks, and approve work plans.[^2][^3]
- Maintenance engineers adjust thresholds in condition monitoring systems and decide when a condition warning justifies creating a CBM task rather than waiting for scheduled PM.[^3][^2]
- Managers review optimization outputs for resource allocation and may override automated allocations based on local knowledge, safety, or operational constraints.[^2][^3]
Human judgement is central: the system provides integrated views and suggestions, but actual task approval and scheduling remain human decisions.

**Inspection trigger**

- CM: triggered by observed defects or failures reported by staff.
- PM: triggered by time‑based schedules.
- CBM: triggered by condition monitoring evidence crossing defined limits or showing adverse trends, which prompts creation of inspection or maintenance tasks linked to BIM assets.[^3][^2]

**Action‑status workflow**

- Requests and system‑generated tasks enter a common project/task pool.
- Tasks are categorized (CM, PM, CBM) and linked to assets and BIM views.[^2][^3]
- Resource optimization algorithms support scheduling but require human confirmation.
- Execution updates asset records and BIM, changing condition status and feeding back into future CBM triggers.[^3][^2]

**Audit trail / transparency**

- All maintenance sources and tasks are normalized and stored as projects/tasks with traceable origins (manual CM vs system PM/CBM).[^2][^3]
- BIM linkage provides a spatial and asset‑level record of where tasks are applied, facilitating post‑hoc audits.[^3][^2]
- Web‑based system records actions, allocations, and status changes; this logs how evidence, rules, and human decisions produced the final plan.

**Validation**

- The paper reports implementation in a Web‑based prototype FMM system, though the ASCE Library abstract does not detail scenario tests; validation is primarily conceptual and via prototype demonstration.[^2][^3]
- No automated damage declaration; rather, the emphasis is on integrating existing maintenance processes and human decisions.

**Directly supported claim and limitation**

- **Claim**: An integrated, BIM‑linked DSS can combine CM, PM, and CBM data into a unified, human‑supervised workflow for facilities maintenance, improving transparency and resource allocation.[^3][^2]
- **Limitations**:
    - Full details of escalation logic and threshold setting are behind a paywall; the abstract confirms integration and human oversight but not specific Normal/Watch/Inspect labels.
    - Optimization of resources is central, so the study edges toward “optimization,” but optimization is constrained and reviewed by human facility managers rather than autonomously declaring damage or actions.
**Reason for inclusion**: Provides a transparent, BIM‑linked, human‑in‑the‑loop CBM framework for buildings, consistent with your chain from monitoring evidence to BIM‑linked components and inspection/maintenance actions.[^2][^3]

## 3. Building Maintenance DSS Focused on Condition Indices and Inspection – IC‑349 DSS vs. Your Escalation Ladder

The BMDSS and the integrated FMM DSS do not explicitly publish a four‑level Normal / Watch / Inspect / Priority Inspection ladder, but both embody escalation through condition thresholds and priority rankings:[^2][^1]

- Inspection ratings and deterioration forecasts determine whether components remain in “normal monitoring” vs. requiring near‑term MR\&R planning (akin to Watch/Inspect).[^1]
- High‑criticality components in poor condition with constrained budgets receive priority MR\&R actions (akin to Priority Inspection/Intervention).[^2][^1]
- All decisions are mediated by asset managers and maintenance engineers, who interpret condition data and decide the level of response rather than allowing the system to declare structural damage or RUL.[^2][^1]

In your PhD chain (vibration → condition/state‑change evidence → BIM‑linked component → inspection record → inspection‑maintenance action support), these frameworks can be seen as examples of the downstream human‑in‑the‑loop decision layer once state‑change evidence is available.

## 4. Fit to your inclusion / exclusion criteria

- **Transparent, human‑in‑the‑loop**: Both DSS frameworks explicitly rely on expert judgement for deterioration modeling, prioritization, threshold setting, and final action approval.[^2][^1]
- **Condition‑based inspection / maintenance**: Condition assessment modules, CBM task generation, and deterioration curves are central to their design.[^2][^1]
- **Escalation levels**: Implemented via condition thresholds, AHP‑weighted indices, and optimization‑based prioritization, even if not labeled with your exact terms.
- **Action‑status workflow and audit trail**: Both track inspection data, maintenance actions, and evolving condition states over time.[^2][^1]
- **Validation**: Methodological validation through references to established infrastructure deterioration models and prototype implementation, with expert involvement rather than autonomous damage declarations.[^2][^1]
- **Exclusions respected**:
    - No RUL/failure‑time prediction used for autonomous decisions; deterioration forecasts are reference tools interpreted by humans.[^1]
    - No autonomous maintenance or automatic damage classification.
    - Optimization is present but framed as a decision aid under human control, not as sole objective.
