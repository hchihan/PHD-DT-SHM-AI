# Field Evidence Base: Vibration Monitoring, Baseline Development, and Low-Cost Sensor Deployment in Steel Structures

This evidence base addresses item 7 of the locked research chain (vibration data → condition/state-change evidence → BIM-linked component → inspection record → inspection-maintenance action support). All entries are screened against the space's scope rules: FEM appears only as nominal/uncalibrated where present, and no entry is used to support calibrated FEM, RUL, stiffness degradation, predictive maintenance, autonomous maintenance, optimal-sensor-placement optimality claims, or direct lab-to-field classifier transfer.

## Classification Legend

- **DIRECT FIELD EVIDENCE** — Field (in-situ, non-laboratory) deployment on a real steel/industrial/long-span structure, reporting deployment protocol details relevant to WP3.
- **STRONG ADJACENT** — Field or near-field study on a related structure type (bridge, non-steel building) with directly transferable methodological content (baseline construction, missing-data handling, environmental variability).
- **METHODOLOGICAL** — Technique/algorithm paper (missing-data recovery, sensor fault isolation, low-cost sensor validation) not itself a full field deployment narrative but methodologically load-bearing for WP3/WP1.
- **OUT OF SCOPE** — Excluded per space rules (lab-only, calibrated FEM/digital twin, direct damage diagnosis without inspection confirmation, lab-to-field classifier transfer).

## Direct Field Evidence

### Earthquake and Ambient Vibration Monitoring of the Steel-Frame UCLA Factor Building

- **Study object:** 17-story moment-resisting steel-frame building (UCLA "Factor" building), real in-service structure.[^1][^2]
- **Sensor type and placement rationale:** Permanently embedded array of 72 accelerometer channels (24-bit digitizers), densely distributed across floor pairs to resolve inter-story dynamic characteristics.[^3][^1]
- **Acquisition duration/protocol:** Continuous recording at 500 Hz; waveform capture spans both ambient (operational) vibration and strong-motion earthquake events over multi-year deployment.[^3]
- **Environmental/operational variability:** Spectral amplitudes show clear daily and weekly patterns correlated with working hours, wind speed, and non-seismic (human-activity) vibration sources; frequency drops correlate with moderate-to-strong shaking events.[^3]
- **Baseline construction:** Ambient-vibration operating baseline established from continuous waveform history, against which transient frequency decreases during shaking are compared.[^1][^3]
- **Mapping to physical components:** Sensor pairs are associated with specific floor levels/inter-story drift; changes in inter-floor dynamic properties are related to system-level property changes (e.g., stiffness loss attributed to a column-related event) via array topology, not FEM calibration.[^3]
- **Practical deployment limitations:** High channel count and high sample rate create substantial data-volume/bandwidth demands, motivating later wireless (Mica-Z/Wisden) sensor-network alternatives noted in the same research program.[^4][^3]
- **Reason for inclusion:** Longest-running, most detailed real steel-frame field deployment identified, with explicit reporting of sensor count, placement logic, sampling protocol, and operational variability sources.
- **Directly supported claim:** Ambient/operational vibration signatures in an occupied steel-frame building are measurably structured by non-structural operational factors (occupancy, wind), which must be separated from any state-change signal before any inspection-triggering inference is drawn.
- **Limitation:** This is a research-grade, densely instrumented, high-cost embedded array; not representative of low-cost sensor economics targeted by WP3. No inspection-record linkage is reported — study stops at signal/frequency change observation.

### Using Embedded Wired and Wireless Seismic Networks in the Moment-Resisting Steel Frame Factor Building for Damage Identification

- **Study object:** Same UCLA Factor building steel frame; wired 72-sensor array plus prototype wireless (Mica-Z/Wisden) sensor mote network.[^3]
- **Sensor type/placement rationale:** Wired embedded accelerometers for permanent monitoring plus a low-power wireless mote network designed to increase spatial resolution without full wired-array cost.[^3]
- **Acquisition protocol:** Continuous 500 Hz waveform recording (wired); wireless network addresses high sample-rate/limited-radio-bandwidth constraints via the Wisden software system.[^3]
- **Environmental/operational variability:** Explicitly reports correlation of vibration amplitude with working hours and wind speed as confounding operational variability sources.[^3]
- **Missing-data/failure handling:** Not detailed for the wired array in this abstract; wireless network design is explicitly motivated by the goal of a more failure-tolerant, scalable alternative to a single dense wired array.[^3]
- **Mapping to components:** Floor-pair sensor comparisons are used to localize dynamic-property change to specific structural regions (paired with, but not calibrated against, an FE model used only for predictive comparison).[^3]
- **Practical limitations:** FE comparison exists in this program but is used for illustrative/predictive comparison, not for diagnosis-grade calibration; the wireless network remained a prototype/testbed rather than an operational deployment at time of publication.
- **Reason for inclusion:** Demonstrates a direct wired-to-wireless low-cost sensor transition case on a real steel structure, relevant to WP3's low-cost sensor rationale.
- **Directly supported claim:** Low-cost wireless sensor motes are a technically motivated substitute for dense wired arrays in real steel-frame monitoring, primarily to improve spatial coverage/cost rather than diagnostic certainty.
- **Limitation:** No inspection-record or BIM-component linkage; damage identification remains at the signal/model-comparison stage, not confirmed by inspection.

## Strong Adjacent Field Evidence

### Long-term health monitoring of concrete and steel bridges under large and missing data by unsupervised meta learning (Entezami et al., 2023)

- **Study object:** Full-scale Z24 concrete box-girder bridge and a full-scale steel arch bridge (KW51), both long-term field-monitored.[^5]
- **Sensor type:** Not itself installed by these authors; uses previously published acceleration-derived natural-frequency datasets from continuous OMA-based field monitoring programs.[^5]
- **Acquisition duration/protocol:** Z24 dataset spans roughly one year of continuous monitoring before demolition; natural frequencies of four modes tracked continuously. KW51 steel arch bridge dataset spans October 2018–January 2020 (before/during/after retrofit), with OMA performed on five-minute ambient-vibration windows.[^5]
- **Environmental/operational variability:** Explicitly documents sharp, non-damage-related frequency increases during freezing weather and other E/O effects requiring correction before any anomaly interpretation.[^5]
- **Baseline construction:** Baseline ("Normal Condition") is built from 75% of pre-event natural-frequency samples; remaining 25% plus post-event samples form the test set, explicitly separating training baseline from inspection/test population.[^5]
- **Missing-data handling:** Explicit missing-value removal reported by mode (e.g., 101–1294 missing samples per mode for Z24) prior to clustering/anomaly-scoring, illustrating a concrete missing-data-handling protocol for long-term field vibration datasets.[^5]
- **Mapping to components:** Frequency features are tracked per structural mode, not per discrete BIM component; this is the main scope gap relative to WP2's BIM-linkage requirement.
- **Practical limitations:** Method requires large historical training baselines (thousands of samples) and retrospective missing-data cleaning rather than real-time failure handling; validated only on bridges, not buildings.
- **Reason for inclusion:** Provides a rigorously documented, field-scale example of long-term baseline construction and missing-data handling directly transferable to WP3's operational-baseline methodology, despite non-building study object.
- **Directly supported claim:** Long-term field vibration monitoring inherently produces missing and environmentally distorted data that must be explicitly filtered before a valid operational baseline can be constructed.
- **Limitation:** Object is bridges, not industrial steel buildings; anomaly output is used by the source study to imply damage detection, which is out of this thesis's scope — only the missing-data/baseline methodology, not the damage-detection conclusion, is imported.

### A novel approach for structural identification and monitoring of a full-scale 17-story building based on ambient vibration measurements

- **Study object:** Same UCLA Factor steel-frame building, focused on time-domain system identification from the permanent 72-channel array.[^1]
- **Sensor type/protocol:** Continuously recorded acceleration data from the embedded dense array.[^1]
- **Reason for inclusion:** Cross-validates sensor configuration and continuous-recording protocol described in the Factor-building direct-evidence entries above, strengthening confidence in the acquisition-protocol details.
- **Directly supported claim:** Time-domain identification techniques are feasible on continuously streamed, permanently embedded field accelerometer data from a real steel high-rise.
- **Limitation:** Identification-methodology focus; does not address BIM linkage, inspection records, or low-cost sensor economics.

## Methodological Evidence (Missing-Data, Sensor-Failure, Low-Cost Sensor Validation)

### Robust SHM: Redundancy approach with different sensor integration levels for long life monitoring systems (Bartels et al., 2024)

- **Study object:** Strain-gauge aging experiment (climate chamber) combined with a small clamped steel cantilever validation model (S235 steel).[^6]
- **Sensor type/placement rationale:** Mixed sensor-integration levels — strain gauges at low-deflection/high-strain locations, accelerometers at high-deflection/low-strain locations — chosen to maximize signal-to-noise ratio per physical quantity measured.[^6]
- **Missing-data/sensor-failure handling:** Central contribution is a two-step redundancy method (hardware redundancy via cross-sensor correlation/Frobenius scalar product; analytical redundancy via Gaussian process regression) to distinguish sensor anomaly from structural anomaly.[^6]
- **Practical deployment limitations:** Validated only on a small steel cantilever with synthetically generated response data layered onto real strain-gauge aging data; explicitly flagged by the authors as needing further work when sensor and structural anomalies coincide.[^6]
- **Reason for inclusion:** Directly informs WP3's missing-data/sensor-failure-handling requirement with a concrete, sensor-fault-vs-structural-change discrimination method applicable to field steel structures.
- **Directly supported claim:** Correlation-based hardware redundancy across differing sensor integration levels can isolate a faulty sensor from a genuine structural signal change without requiring a calibrated structural model.
- **Limitation:** Uses a nominal FEM-discretized cantilever only to generate synthetic virtual-sensor data for validation, not as a diagnostic tool; the FEM use here is illustrative/reference and must not be over-cited as field validation. Structure size is far below industrial-building scale.

### A Low-Cost Wireless Multinode Vibration Monitoring System for Civil Structures

- **Study object:** Arduino-based multinode wireless vibration system validated on a real-scale concrete slab (not steel) alongside a high-grade reference system.[^7]
- **Sensor type/placement rationale:** Low-cost MEMS-based nodes chosen explicitly for scalability and non-specialist reproducibility; hardware/source code disclosed openly.[^7]
- **Acquisition protocol:** Impulse and ambient-vibration acquisition tests; sampling-frequency stability characterized and corrected via interpolation post-processing.[^7]
- **Practical deployment limitations:** Explicitly reports that the low-cost system produced usable results in impulse (forced) tests but failed to produce usable data under ambient (operational) vibration conditions — a critical, directly relevant limitation for any low-cost operational-baseline deployment.[^7]
- **Reason for inclusion:** Provides a rare, explicit negative finding on low-cost sensor performance under ambient/operational conditions, which is a core risk factor for WP3's field feasibility claims.
- **Directly supported claim:** Off-the-shelf low-cost MEMS/Arduino sensor nodes can match high-grade systems in impulse-response tests but may lack sufficient sensitivity for ambient (unforced) operational vibration monitoring, directly bounding field feasibility claims.
- **Limitation:** Structure tested is a concrete slab, not a steel frame; no industrial building or BIM/inspection linkage.

### A Sensor Network with Embedded Data Processing and Data-to-Cloud Capabilities for Vibration-Based Real-Time SHM

- **Study object:** Network of five low-power/low-cost triaxial MEMS accelerometer nodes tested on a simply supported steel beam (laboratory-scale) with cloud data transmission.[^8]
- **Sensor type/placement rationale:** Nodes selected for minimized power consumption (<300 mW) and weight (<50 g total network) enabling remote, continuous field-style operation.[^8]
- **Reason for inclusion:** Establishes low-cost-node power/weight/data-transmission benchmarks (edge processing plus cloud upload) directly relevant to WP3's low-cost sensor deployment rationale, even though the validation structure is a laboratory steel beam rather than a field building.
- **Directly supported claim:** Ultra-low-power MEMS sensor nodes with embedded spectral processing and cloud transmission are technically viable for continuous, unattended vibration monitoring of steel members.
- **Limitation:** Laboratory-only validation (free-vibration simply supported beam); no field deployment, no environmental/operational variability, no missing-data handling reported. Classified methodological rather than field evidence because of the lab-only test object.

### Condition monitoring of critical industrial assets using high performing low-cost MEMS accelerometers

- **Study object:** Industrial rotating/critical assets (not building structural frames) monitored with low-cost MEMS accelerometers.[^9]
- **Reason for inclusion:** Provides a benchmark for low-cost MEMS accelerometer performance claims in an industrial (not laboratory) setting, supporting sensor-selection rationale even though the object is machinery rather than a steel building frame.
- **Directly supported claim:** Low-cost MEMS accelerometers can achieve performance suitable for industrial condition-monitoring contexts under real operating environments.
- **Limitation:** Application domain is rotating machinery, not building/structural components; not directly transferable to structural sensor-to-BIM-component mapping.

## Synthesis for WP3 (Field Feasibility)

No paper identified in this search fully satisfies all seven requested reporting dimensions (sensor type/placement rationale, acquisition protocol, environmental/operational variability, baseline construction, missing-data handling, sensor-to-component mapping, deployment limitations) simultaneously for an industrial steel building. The UCLA Factor building studies are the strongest direct match for structure type and operational-variability reporting but predate low-cost sensor economics and use dense, expensive, permanently embedded arrays rather than the low-cost configurations WP3 targets. The Entezami et al. bridge study is the strongest match for explicit missing-data and baseline-construction methodology but is non-building and non-steel-frame in structural typology. The low-cost sensor node studies (Arduino multinode system, cloud-connected MEMS network) establish sensor-selection and power/cost benchmarks but stop at laboratory or non-steel validation and, in one case, explicitly report failure under ambient/operational conditions — a material risk finding for WP3's operational-baseline feasibility claim. No source in this search reports an end-to-end Sensor_ID-to-BIM_GUID mapping workflow at a real industrial steel building; this remains an identified evidence gap for WP3, consistent with the locked scope's prohibition on claiming a literature gap from search summaries alone — this statement should be treated as a search-limited observation pending a dedicated systematic search, not a confirmed literature gap.[^2][^8][^7][^1][^5][^3]
