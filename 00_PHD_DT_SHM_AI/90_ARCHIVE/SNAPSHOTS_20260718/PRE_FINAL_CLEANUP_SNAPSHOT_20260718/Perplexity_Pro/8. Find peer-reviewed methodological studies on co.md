# 8. Find peer-reviewed methodological studies on constructing operational baselines

from vibration or accelerometer data for in-service civil structures when confirmed
damage labels are unavailable.

Focus on:

- baseline reference periods;
- environmental and operational variability;
- robust or adaptive thresholds;
- sensor drift and recalibration;
- data quality and missing-data rules;
- anomaly-to-inspection escalation without making a damage diagnosis.

Exclude RUL, predictive maintenance, automated diagnosis, and weighted health indices.

For each study report:
study object; baseline method; minimum monitoring duration; threshold logic;
validation method; human inspection role; and limitation.

The most relevant peer‑reviewed work gives you unsupervised, threshold‑based baselining from vibration (or closely related SHM responses) where only “intact” data are available and anomalies trigger further investigation rather than automated diagnosis. Below I summarise five methodological exemplars and then pull out design lessons for your PhD architecture.

***

## 1. Vamvoudakis‑Stefanou et al. 2018 – Population‑based unsupervised baselines

This paper develops an unsupervised multiple‑model statistical time‑series approach for vibration‑based damage detection on a *population* of nominally identical structures, trained only on intact data.[^1][^2]

- **Study object**
Nominally identical civil structures (concept is illustrated with vibration response time series from multiple similar structures; the framework is generic but motivated by civil SHM).[^2][^1]
- **Baseline method**
Multiple statistical time‑series models are fitted to scalar vibration response signals from each sampled intact structure, building a reference “population baseline” that captures normal variability across members.[^1][^2]
In operation, new signals are evaluated against this ensemble; deviations in likelihood across models indicate anomalies.
- **Minimum monitoring duration**
The training phase assumes sufficiently long intact vibration records for each sample structure to robustly estimate model parameters and cross‑structure variability; practically this implies at least several campaigns per structure (hours to days per campaign) rather than single short runs.[^2][^1]
- **Threshold logic**
Thresholds are defined in terms of detection statistics derived from the multiple‑model likelihoods (e.g. decision boundaries on posterior model probabilities or residuals), tuned to control false alarms given the population variability.[^1][^2]
No damage labels are used – thresholds are set statistically over intact data.
- **Validation method**
Validation is by simulation and controlled perturbation: damaged states are synthetically or experimentally created for some structures and the method’s detection performance (hit rate, false alarm) is evaluated against these.[^2][^1]
- **Human inspection role**
Detected anomalies are framed as “possible damage” requiring further engineering investigation; the algorithm does not localise or diagnose damage mechanisms.[^1][^2]
- **Limitation**
    - Requires a population of similar structures with comparable response characteristics – less applicable to unique one‑off bridges.[^1]
    - Focuses on detection performance, not on detailed rules for sensor drift, missing data, or escalation workflows. These aspects are not formalised.[^2][^1]

**Reason for inclusion:**
Methodological evidence – clean unsupervised baseline construction from intact vibration data with explicit consideration of structural and environmental variability across a population.

## 2. Tan et al. 2023 – Dynamic thresholds for a cable‑stayed bridge

This work proposes a dynamic early‑warning scheme for a long‑span cable‑stayed bridge, based on benchmark thresholds modulated by temperature and operational conditions.[^3][^4]

- **Study object**
In‑service cable‑stayed bridge with SHM instrumentation (strain, displacement, or vibration‑related response; temperature sensors are also used).[^4]
- **Baseline method**
    - A “benchmark threshold” is first determined from historical intact data for key response indicators (e.g. stay‑cable forces, displacements).[^4]
    - Temperature effects are explicitly modelled; the dynamic threshold at any time is obtained by combining the benchmark value with the predicted temperature‑dependent response.[^5][^4]
- **Minimum monitoring duration**
Baseline construction uses multi‑year historical SHM data to capture seasonal temperature cycles and operational variability; the paper stresses that at least one full annual cycle is needed for reliable temperature baselines.[^5][^4]
- **Threshold logic**
    - Static benchmark thresholds (e.g. percentile‑based limits) are established from intact data.
    - Real‑time measurements are compared to temperature‑adjusted dynamic thresholds; exceedances trigger warnings.[^5][^4]
    - Thresholds are set to balance sensitivity and false alarms, with separate levels for “attention” vs “alarm”.[^3][^4]
- **Validation method**
    - Historical case data with known extreme events and simulated perturbations are used to show that dynamic thresholds reduce false alarms compared to static limits under varying temperature.[^4][^5]
- **Human inspection role**
Exceedance of dynamic thresholds triggers engineering assessment and, where necessary, site inspection; the system provides early warning but does not diagnose damage.[^4]
- **Limitation**
    - Focused on temperature variability; other operational factors (traffic pattern changes, sensor drift) are treated more heuristically.[^5][^4]
    - Data quality rules (missing data, recalibration) are not fully formalised; sensor maintenance is assumed rather than modelled.[^5][^4]

**Reason for inclusion:**
Direct methodological evidence on baseline reference periods, environmental variability, and dynamic thresholds for an in‑service civil structure.

## 3. “Development of a baseline for SHM for a curved post‑tensioned concrete box–girder bridge” – 2009

This case study develops a baseline response for a unique curved post‑tensioned concrete box–girder bridge using field tests and finite‑element–assisted interpretation, focusing on long‑term monitoring without labelled damage events.[^6]

- **Study object**
Single in‑service curved post‑tensioned concrete box‑girder bridge instrumented for long‑term SHM.[^6]
- **Baseline method**
    - Field testing data (controlled load tests and ambient vibration) plus finite element (FE) simulations are used to establish the initial baseline response envelope for key metrics (natural frequencies, modal shapes, strains, displacements).[^6]
    - Subsequent monitoring data are compared to this envelope to detect deviations outside expected variability.[^6]
- **Minimum monitoring duration**
Baseline includes both initial commissioning tests and an extended intact monitoring period to characterise variability; the paper implies at least months of data post‑commissioning before formal thresholds are set.[^6]
- **Threshold logic**
    - Acceptable ranges for frequencies and strains are derived from FE and intact field data; exceedances beyond specified percentage deviations from baseline are flagged.[^6]
    - Thresholds are static and tied to engineering judgement and code‑based limits rather than purely data‑driven statistics.[^6]
- **Validation method**
    - Sensitivity studies using FE simulations of possible damage scenarios (e.g. tendon loss, cracking) show how these would manifest in monitored parameters; this tests whether baseline‑derived thresholds would be exceeded.[^6]
- **Human inspection role**
Any persistent exceedance or anomalous trend leads to a detailed engineering assessment and, if warranted, physical inspection; there is no automated damage diagnosis.[^6]
- **Limitation**
    - Strong dependence on FE modelling and engineering heuristics; environmental and operational variability (traffic, temperature) are acknowledged but not modelled statistically.[^6]
    - Sensor drift and missing data handling are largely procedural, not codified as formal rules.[^6]

**Reason for inclusion:**
Background methodological evidence on baseline development for a unique civil structure, showing how FE‑assisted baselines can be used for anomaly escalation without explicit damage labels—relevant as “baseline reference period + envelope” precedent, but your PhD must keep FEM nominal.[^7][^6]

## 4. Temperature‑based SHM baseline for long‑span bridges – 2015

This study proposes explicit separation and modelling of temperature effects to build a baseline response for long‑span bridges, again without damage labels.[^5]

- **Study object**
Long‑span bridge(s) with continuous monitoring of structural response (e.g. frequencies, strains) and temperature.[^5]
- **Baseline method**
    - Structural response is decomposed into temperature‑dependent and residual components using regression or machine‑learning models trained on intact data.[^5]
    - The temperature‑based model defines the expected response at any temperature; anomalies are defined on the residuals.[^5]
- **Minimum monitoring duration**
The method requires long‑term intact data spanning a full annual temperature cycle (preferably multiple years) to reliably identify temperature–response relationships.[^5]
- **Threshold logic**
    - Thresholds are set on residuals between measured and temperature‑predicted response, typically via statistical limits (e.g. mean ± k·standard deviation or quantile‑based bounds).[^5]
    - This cleanly separates environmental variability from potential structural changes.[^5]
- **Validation method**
    - Historical data with known events and simulated damage scenarios (through modified properties in FE or synthetic perturbations) are used to test anomaly detection efficacy.[^5]
- **Human inspection role**
Exceedance of residual thresholds is treated as an alert that prompts investigation; the method does not immediately classify damage type or severity.[^5]
- **Limitation**
    - Focused on temperature; other operational factors (traffic loading, wind) are either secondary or assumed random.[^5]
    - Sensor drift is not explicitly modelled; recalibration is assumed to keep sensors consistent with the baseline.[^5]

**Reason for inclusion:**
Methodological evidence on disentangling environmental variability from genuine structural change, directly relevant to your baseline and threshold design.

## 5. Hybrid CAE–DSVDD unsupervised vibration anomaly detection – 2025

This recent paper proposes an unsupervised anomaly detection framework that uses vibration data from intact structures only, combining computer‑aided engineering (CAE) outputs with a deep support vector data description (DSVDD) type boundary.[^8][^2]

- **Study object**
Vibration response of civil structures (bridges/buildings) where only intact SHM data are available for training; CAE models are used to characterise expected response space.[^8]
- **Baseline method**
    - CAE simulations and intact measurements define the “normal” feature space of vibration responses (e.g. modal features, time‑series embeddings).[^8]
    - A DSVDD (deep one‑class boundary) is trained on these, enclosing the normal data region.[^8][^2]
- **Minimum monitoring duration**
Adequate intact monitoring data are needed to populate the feature space; the paper assumes multi‑campaign data rather than single short tests, plus CAE scenarios.[^8][^2]
- **Threshold logic**
    - The one‑class boundary itself functions as a robust threshold: points outside the learned hypersphere are anomalies.[^2][^8]
    - Hyperparameters are tuned to trade off between false alarms and missed anomalies, using validation subsets of intact data.[^8][^2]
- **Validation method**
    - Synthetic and experimental damage scenarios are introduced and their feature points evaluated against the boundary to demonstrate detection performance.[^2][^8]
- **Human inspection role**
Detected anomalies are flags, not diagnoses; the framework is framed as early warning requiring engineering interpretation and possibly inspection.[^8]
- **Limitation**
    - Relies on CAE, which moves towards calibrated FEM; for your PhD this must be reframed as *nominal, reference‑only*.[^7][^8]
    - Does not elaborate operational rules for sensor drift, missing data, or escalation workflows; focus is on algorithm performance.

**Reason for inclusion:**
Methodological evidence on unsupervised vibration‑based baselines where only intact data define a one‑class boundary; directly relevant to robust/anomaly thresholds without labels, but CAE coupling must be de‑emphasised in your limited‑FEM scope.

## 6. Threshold values for SHM using GMM and decision boundaries

A chapter on “Establishing threshold values for use in structural health monitoring” develops statistical threshold‑setting from live‑load strain data on an instrumented cable‑stayed bridge.[^9]

- **Study object**
Instrumented cable‑stayed bridge with live‑load strain monitoring.[^9]
- **Baseline method**
    - Gaussian mixture models (GMM) are fitted to recorded strain measurements from intact operation, using AICc for model selection.[^9]
    - Baseline thresholds correspond to upper specific limits (USLs) of the GMM components (e.g. 99% USLs).[^9]
- **Minimum monitoring duration**
The method assumes extensive intact strain data under representative traffic to fit GMMs; practically, this implies many months of monitoring campaigns.[^9]
- **Threshold logic**
    - Threshold strain values are selected from GMM USLs.
    - Damage indices (DIs) are defined via outlier ratios (ORs), and decision boundaries (DBs) are derived using t‑distributions to differentiate minor vs significant abnormal responses.[^9]
    - No damage labels are needed; thresholds and DBs come purely from intact statistics and simulated perturbations.[^9]
- **Validation method**
    - Simulated abnormal responses are created by perturbing actual SHM data; sensitivity of DIs and DBs to minor and significant abnormal response is assessed.[^9]
- **Human inspection role**
DIs and DBs are intended to guide bridge owners’ decisions; exceeding DB thresholds leads to engineering evaluation and potential inspection, not automatic diagnosis.[^9]
- **Limitation**
    - Focused on strain, not vibration; extension to accelerometers is conceptual rather than demonstrated.[^9]
    - Sensor drift, missing data handling, and recalibration rules are not central topics; they are assumed controlled by SHM operations.[^9]

**Reason for inclusion:**
Strong adjacent methodological evidence on threshold logic, statistical baselines, and decision boundaries for escalation.

## Cross‑Study Design Lessons for Your Baseline

### Baseline reference periods

- All studies stress that baselines must be built from **extended intact periods**, often at least one annual cycle to capture environmental variability.[^4][^6][^5]
- Population‑based methods benefit from multiple structures’ intact data, but for unique bridges, you must rely on multi‑month/annual single‑structure data plus reference models.[^1][^6]


### Environmental and operational variability

- Temperature is consistently treated as a primary driver, modelled explicitly in temperature‑based baselines and dynamic thresholds.[^4][^5]
- Traffic and other operational loads are largely handled statistically (mixture models, time‑series variability) rather than mechanistically; you may adopt similar distribution‑based baselines for accelerometer features.[^1][^2][^9]


### Robust/adaptive thresholds

- Approaches include:
    - One‑class boundaries (DSVDD, isolation‑forest‑style) trained on intact vibration data.[^10][^2][^8]
    - Statistical USLs and decision boundaries from GMMs and t‑distributions.[^9]
    - Dynamic thresholds modulated by temperature and other covariates.[^4][^5]
- Common pattern: thresholds are derived from intact data plus a separate validation subset or simulation, not from labelled damage runs.


### Sensor drift and recalibration

- None of the highlighted papers provide detailed sensor‑drift modelling; drift is typically treated as a maintenance issue, with recalibration assumed.[^4][^6][^5]
- For your PhD, you may need to specify **operational rules**: e.g. drift detection via baseline shifts in calibration channels, triggering sensor maintenance before structural anomaly interpretation.


### Data quality and missing‑data rules

- Case‑study baselines mention consistency checks and exclusion of periods with sensor faults but do not formalise missing‑data rules.[^4][^6][^5]
- Unsupervised ML reviews emphasise the importance of clean intact data yet provide limited operational guidance.[^2]
- You will likely need explicit rules (minimum percentage of data per window, imputation vs discard policies) – this remains a gap, flagged already in your living evidence base.[^7]


### Anomaly‑to‑inspection escalation

- All methods **stop at anomaly detection** and frame outputs as triggers for engineering judgement, rating updates, or inspection.[^1][^4][^6][^9][^5]
- None implement full automatic damage diagnosis or RUL; this aligns well with your “any anomaly triggers inspection, not a damage claim” boundary.[^7]
